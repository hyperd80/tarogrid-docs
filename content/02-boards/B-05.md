**Goal:** run a staged process — orders, leads, content — by physically moving cards, and understand what a drag really does.

:::demo kanban-drag · "The drag is an edit" · 12s
Drag a card from Pending to Confirmed → the Table's status cell flips too. One truth, many glasses.
:::

## How Kanban thinks

Kanban shows your rows as **cards**, in columns built from a Status-type field's values: *Pending / Confirmed / Shipped*. Dragging a card to another column **edits that field** — it's not a visual shuffle; it's the same edit you'd make in the Table, wearing friendlier clothes ([views principle, B-03](#/B-03)).

That means everything watching the field reacts: the Table shows the new value, and an automation watching the status fires — drag an order into *Confirmed* and [the dispatch recipe books the courier](#/S-16).

## Reading the wall

A Kanban is a *flow x-ray*: a bulging column is a bottleneck ("why are nine things in Packing?"), an empty one is starvation. You'll spot process problems here that a Table hides in plain rows.

:::tip
Cards show a few key fields — pick the ones a glance needs (customer, total, the fraud light). The full story stays one click away in the [Item panel](#/B-02).
:::

## Pitfalls

- ⚠️ A drag is a real edit with real consequences — the robot watching the column can't tell a decision from a tidy-up ([S-16](#/S-16)'s warning, in view form).
- Don't create stages like "Misc" or "Later-later" — stages you wouldn't say out loud become card graveyards.

## Example

:::example
Ben runs Maya's content on a Kanban: *Idea / Drafting / Ready / Posted*. Monday he drags three cards to Ready; Maya glances at the wall Tuesday — Drafting is bulging, Ideas are thin — and knows exactly what the week needs without a meeting.
:::
